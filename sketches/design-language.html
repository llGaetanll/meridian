Design language

Every control Meridian draws, once, so the phone, the web and the desktop app draw the same thing. The styles are in sketches/shared/components.css and the colours in sketches/shared/theme.css; nothing on this page restyles a component, so if one looks wrong here it is wrong.

Two ideas run through all of it. Anything you can select is a frame: an outer hairline, three pixels of ground, and the fill inset inside that — so the selected state is a thing sitting in a frame, not a thing whose border changed colour. Anything that does something stands on a solid line of its own darker shade and drops onto it when pressed. If it stands up it does something; if it lies flat it says something.

Everything is scoped to .ui here so new controls can be drawn beside old ones without the two fighting. In the app there is no such scope — the class names are the components.

Three jobs, and three greys

Violet is you: what you picked, where you are, what day it is. Lime and rose are money in and money owed, and only ever on a figure — never on the word beside it, which already says the direction. Every other thing on the screen is one of three greys.

The app used to carry sixteen category hues as well, one per Plaid primary, in filled capsules down the left of the ledger. They came out: every screen that used one says the category in words in the same place the colour used to sit, and the palette above is what is left. Categorical hues are for a chart where categories are being compared, not for labelling a row.

Two themes

The dark theme is the app's, and it is what anything gets if it says nothing. The light one arrives on data-theme="light" — on the page, or on any one thing in it, which is why every control on this page is drawn twice: dark on the left, the same control light beside it, and the words about it on the page's own black.

A screen never names a shade. --violet-300 is a word in violet on black and a watermark on paper, so the vocabulary a screen may use is roles — --bg, --text, --accent-text, --up — and a theme is nothing but those roles set a second time. Two controls side by side is the test: a colour that holds in one and fails in the other is a shade that got named somewhere it should not have been.

Three things in the light theme are not an inversion. The greens and reds move a long way down their ramps, because on paper a colour has to be dark to be a colour at all. The accent splits in two: the fill goes one step darker so white still sits on it, and the lettering goes darker still. And the grounds change order — the page is the grey one and a panel is the white one, because a card in a lit room is paper on a desk rather than a light in a cellar.

The glow

Anything holding the highlight colour sits in a little of it — --glow, a short shadow falling downward. Small enough that you would not name it if asked what changed.

It falls downward rather than haloing because a blurred shadow at no offset rounds the corners off: a rectangle wearing one reads as a lozenge, dimmer at the corners than along the edges. And it goes on single things, never on a row of them — nine glowing chips is nine little lamps.

Two weights, both full width

.rule-sec between the parts of a page, .rule-row between rows and between groups of them. Both run to the edge of the screen.

A rule that stops short of the edge reads as a third kind of line, which is how a page ends up looking like it has three systems in it. Rows you read down rather than compare across — transactions, accounts — get space and a heading instead of either.

A rule with a word in it

.rule-word between two ways of doing the same job. The word is the whole control: without it a rule says these are different things, which is the opposite of what is meant.

First run's front door and the paywall both offer several ways through, and a plain stack of buttons reads as a list of chores rather than a choice. Only where the alternative is genuine — never to break a form into steps.

Selecting

Chip

The frame, at its usual size. Selected is the accent tint with the label in violet-300 and the border warmed by the same amount. A count goes at the left of the label, in the money face. .sm is the same thing smaller, for a summary rather than a control you aim at; .dead is drawn but not built; .empty is a category nothing matches, dimmed the same way but still there to tap.

The label sits in .lb, a slot as wide as the label set semibold, with the text repeated in data-label. Selecting a chip sets its label semibold, which is a pixel or two wider — enough to re-pack a row of chips and move every one of them under the pointer. With the slot, a chip you tap stays where it was.
.chip > .in > (.n, .lb[data-label]) · .on · .sm · .dead · .empty

Chip row

A justified paragraph, which is what it is. Every line but the last is as full as it can be, and the space between its chips takes up whatever is left so the line is flush at both edges. The line with the most room to spare is the one put last, where it sits left at its natural spacing.

The packing leaves that loosest line out of what it costs, so the remainder is pushed onto one line rather than shared out over all of them and the lines that are left come out tight. Stretching a short line across the measure is the ragged edge you can see from across the room, and it is why the last line of a paragraph is not justified either. A line with one chip on it is never stretched at all.

The chips keep their own widths — stretching them makes “Travel” as wide as “Transportation”, and a chip's width should say something about its label.

This is the core's job, not the view's: the platform receives the order already packed, like every other finished value. ChipRows.swift is where the rule is written down.

Range

One outline, the options inside it, the live one filled — the web's 1D/1W/1M selector, and the same control stood on its end. Every option the same height. .tok is an editable word inside one.

The UI of an enum. Outlining each option separately makes four controls sitting near each other rather than one control with four settings, and rows of different heights say one case is bigger than another, which in an enum it is not.

Acting

Button

Raised on a solid line of its own darker shade, and it drops onto that line when pressed. Solid violet is one action per screen: the thing you are there to do. Everything else is the quiet one, and .dead is the chip's own drawn-but-not-built state — the app has two of those already, Reconnect and the dock's search. .warn letters a button amber: an action the app is asking for rather than offering, which so far is only a login that has expired, and which is plain — a login is a trip out to the bank's own page, not a thing that happens here.

The edge has to be darker than the button and lighter than the page, or it disappears against one of them — which is why the quiet button sits a step up from the ground it stands on rather than flush with it. Both are lit from the middle of their base, and the glow is measured in the button's own width and height, so it is the shape of whatever it is lighting: a wide button gets a wide one and Retry gets a small one, and neither reaches its corners. A gradient run top to bottom drew a line across the top edge whatever its steps were set to; this reads as one colour catching the light. The violet one carries it openly because it is the action, the quiet one about half as far. There is one button size: a smaller one for a lesser place made the same action look like a lesser action, which is not what a banner's Retry is. The amber one is lettered rather than filled: --warn is a light amber on black and a dark one on paper, so a filled one would need its text colour set twice.

Large, and across the measure

.lg grows the type and the padding, .block takes the width of whatever it is in. The phone's one action per screen is both; a row of buttons beside a list is neither.

The edge, the corner and the violet stay exactly as they are, so this is the same button rather than a second one for phones. A control that has a different shape on every platform is three controls to keep in agreement.
.btn.primary.lg.block

A button that is not ours

.theirs is the button somebody else draws. Apple and Google hand out their sign-in buttons; Stripe renders Apple Pay, Google Pay and Link inside its own frame. Each wears its owner's face and its owner's words, down to the wordmark and the four-colour G.

Every one of those rulebooks says the same thing, and none of them is optional: the button looks like theirs or it does not ship. Somebody hunting for the Apple Pay button they know will not find a violet one, and a violet one is the only kind we could have made. So the sheet carries them, and a screen holding three of them is designed around what they really look like rather than what we wish they did. Never style anything else this way, and always put a rule between them and a real action of ours.
These are drawn here so a screen can be laid out, not so they can be built: on the web they arrive inside Stripe's Express Checkout Element, and the only things we choose are the height, the corner radius, each button's theme and its wording. Which of them appear at all is decided by the browser and by what the customer has set up, so a screen that only works with three buttons on it does not work.
.btn.theirs.lg.block · .link

Working

A press that landed and has not finished. The label says what is being waited on rather than "Loading", and the button stops taking taps while it waits.

Not .dead, which is a button there is no point pressing. This one was pressed. The phone draws it on Connect a bank: the server opens a Plaid session and iOS raises a web sheet over the app, about a second between the tap and anything appearing, and a second of nothing reads as a tap that missed. The glyph is the arc the sync rows turn, at the same speed — one spinner in the app rather than each platform reaching for its own.
.btn.busy

Button group

The same buttons with the gap taken out: shared side borders, and the edges running into one line under the row. The live one is the violet button exactly.

A row of flat panels with one of them filled is a segment, which is a different control that happens to look similar. This is a row of buttons with one of them pressed, and it should keep saying so.

Plain button

No frame and no edge, so it presses down a pixel of its own. For an action that has to be available without being the loudest thing in its row — Reconnect, sitting in a list of bank names.

Under the pointer it takes a wash of the colour it is already lettered in — enough to say it is a button, not enough to give it the frame the quiet one has. An icon here is worth its space when it says something the label cannot: “open in new” on Reconnect is true, because Plaid Link in update mode is a hosted page and the tap leaves the app.
.btn.plain (:hover)

Back

The plain button holding only the chevron, above the step on a screen of a flow. Grey rather than violet, and the glyph at the size of a word rather than a button's.

A screen with no title bar has nowhere else to put it, and one chevron does not earn a bar. Grey because going back is never what the screen is for — the violet is the way forward. Where it goes is the machine's call, so on every platform it is an event and not a history pop.
.btn.plain.back > svg

Menu

What you can do to the thing you pressed, as a card of flat rows with a glyph at the left of each. .cut groups them: what you set, then the ways out. A row that is a setting rather than a door carries what it is set to at the right, in .val, and pressing the row moves it — the two halves above say Dark and Light because each is showing the theme it is drawn in.

Rows and not buttons. Every line in here does something, so buttons would make the card a stack of raised things with no answer among them; they lie flat and light under the pointer, which is a row's behaviour. Nothing animates, because the card does not arrive from anywhere — it is the frame that the control you pressed is now sitting inside.
.grave is here too, in rose, for a row that cannot be undone. A bare row is a thin place to ask something irreversible, so where the menu is the question it says so first: .say is the question at the head of the card, the bold line what is being asked and the grey line what it costs, and the answer is .acts — the card's own two rows, side by side under the rule, safe on the left. That is the second card above, which is what removing one bank opens. Side by side and not stacked, because two answers to one question are not a list of two things you might do.
Asked in the card rather than over the page, because one bank of four is not something the whole window has to stop for — and the card stands under the button that was pressed, which is what says which bank without the question having to be read to find out. Deleting an account is the other way round: that one stops everything, because there is nothing behind it to come back to. .fault is where a refused answer is printed, in the card, so the question is still there to answer again.
.menu > (.say > b, .fault, .acts > .item(.grave), .item > .val, .cut)

Destructive button

The quiet button in rose: the same frame as every other, lettered and edged in the colour that means there is nothing behind the door. Beside it, the same action where it sits on a row rather than at the foot of a page — removing one bank of four — which is .plain.mute: no frame, a glyph and no word, and grey.

Never the violet one. The loud button on a screen is the thing you came to do, and deleting your account is never that — it is the thing you came to do once, and it is asked again before it happens. Rose says which of the two buttons in that question is the one that cannot be taken back. That is also why the one on a row is not rose: four rose trash cans down a list of banks would make the loudest thing on the page the thing nobody came to do, and the card it opens asks again in rose anyway. Grey and glyph-only for the same reason — it carries its words for a screen reader.
.btn.grave · .btn.plain.mute (:hover)

Dialog

A window in the middle of the app over a dimmed page. Two shapes and one card: the wide one has a rail down its left with a row per page, and .ask is the narrow one with no rail, for a dialog that asks one question. Above, the settings window and the confirmation that stands over it.

The page stays where it is. What a dialog holds belongs to the account and not to the page underneath, so taking somebody off the ledger to change their name is a navigation they then have to undo.
The rail's rows are the menu's rows exactly, because they are the same thing: a flat line with a glyph that does something when pressed. One page on it is fine — the rail is drawn so that the second page is a row added to a window that already has a shape. The row you are on is lit by its ground and not by a colour: a rail with one page on it would otherwise have a violet row in it at all times. The rail names the page, so the pane does not head itself with the same two words — what is at the top of the pane is the way out of it.
The one thing the page is for stands at the foot, drawn in both states and dead until there is something to write: a button that appears on the first keystroke is a button that was not there to be aimed at. It fades up and does not travel: a window over everything has nowhere to have come from. Three ways out — the scrim, the cross, and Escape — because a window with a field in it is one people press Escape at. The actions run along the bottom rather than stacking, and the loud one is the safe one.
.scrim > .dialog(.ask) > (.rail > (.rail-head, .item.on), .pane > (.pane-head > .shut, .setting > .setting-row, .pane-foot > .pane-acts, .acts))

A title, a line or two under it, and the actions at the foot. First run is a stack of these and so is the paywall. The measure is short because these are sentences: a line of prose run across a 900px page is one the eye loses its place in.

A screen in a flow

The same block with the parts a flow adds. .say-step above the title says where you are; .say-fault says what went wrong, under the thing it went wrong with; .say-note is a quiet line under the actions, and may carry a plain button; .say-legal is small print at the foot.

The step is the only reason a flow says how long it is. The fault is absent until something has actually gone wrong — never a warning waiting in advance — and its colour is the field's .bad, so the two read as one event. The note is where a way out sits when it must be there without being the loudest thing: the countdown to a new code, and the address one click from being fixed. The small print goes at the point of consent rather than in a settings screen, because that is where the data starts moving.

First run is where these came from, and they are .onb-step, .err, .resend and .legal in sketches/mobile/phone.css. A title keeps the line breaks it was written with.

An outcome

A glyph beside the title, for a screen that is the result of something rather than a step towards it: the tick where it finished, the warning where it did not.

The glyph carries the colour so the title does not have to. It sits on the title's line because it is an adjective on the sentence, not a thing of its own — stacked over it, the eye stopped twice. The two words are the row's, .done and .needs, so a colour means the same thing on a row and beside a title.

Banner

Something went wrong, in the machine's words, and the one thing to do about it. It sits at the top of the page, over whatever was loaded before, and goes when the next load lands.

A banner rather than a screen: the ledger from a minute ago is still worth reading while the server is away. Field

The frame again, at the size of a thing you type into: the hairline, three pixels of ground, and the well inset in them. Typing in it warms the frame by exactly what selecting warms a chip's.

So a live field and a chosen chip are the same event happening on screen, and violet keeps meaning you. .bad is for a value the server rejected — an expired code, an address that is not one — and never for an empty field: nothing has gone wrong yet, and a form that turns red as you arrive is scolding you for showing up.
A field that really takes typing holds an input in place of the value, wearing none of its own chrome, and its placeholder wears .ph's grey.
.field > .in > (svg, value | .ph | input, .caret) · .on · .bad

Code

As many fields as there are digits, one glyph each, set in the money face. The field that takes the next digit is the live one.

Monospace because a code is read a digit at a time and every glyph wants its own slot — the same reason a column of figures is set in it. And a box per digit says how many are left without a counter saying so.

Where it takes typing, one real input sits behind all the boxes, invisible and on top of them, rather than one per box. A paste, a backspace and a code filled in from the notification are then one event rather than a question of where focus moves, and the boxes draw what it holds.

Card

Three fields: the number across the measure, and the date and the code side by side under it, in the money face. Under them, when a payment is refused, the sentence saying why; while one is out, the whole card dims and stops taking typing.

Three rather than one long field, so each is as wide as what it holds, and monospace for the reason a code is: a card number is read four digits at a time, and each group keeps its slot.

On the web and the desktop the wells are empty and Stripe's frames sit in them, so a card number never passes through the app. A frame has no input of ours to read, so it marks the element it sits in with .on or .bad, and the field lights from that exactly as it does for .field.on. .bad is Stripe saying a number cannot be one — not an empty field. The phone draws no card: it sells through the App Store.

Row

An icon, a name, a second line under it, and something on the right. Every list in the app that is not transactions is this one: what connecting a bank means, what the first sync has fetched, what a connection needs, what is in an account.

A list of facts and a list of settings are the same shape, so there is one row and not one per screen — three screens each inventing a row is how the same list ends up three heights. .top is for a row whose second line is a sentence rather than a value, and lifts the icon to the first line instead of the middle. .flat drops the icon and the weight for a name and a figure read down a column. .needs turns the second line amber, for the one row in a list that wants doing something about. .mask boxes an account's last four digits at the head of its second line, monospaced: it is a number off a card rather than a word, and it is what tells two checking accounts apart. .stale greys a row whole: still true as far as anyone knows, and no longer arriving.

The month, run sideways, with a column of the month either side showing at the edges. A swipe turns it; the names over it travel with the grid, the one nearest the middle brightest and largest.

Turning it is a drag rather than a redraw: all three months are drawn a page apart and the swipe moves the lot, so the month you are turning towards is already there at the edge before you turn to it. The page is carried over first and the machine told after, so the new month is drawn in the pixels the old one has just left. Each month is as many rows as it has weeks, and the grid grows or shrinks a row as the months turn rather than jumping.

Mark

Who a row is: a bank's logo on Connections, a merchant's in the ledger. One square, whether the logo arrived or not, so the text of every row starts on the same line either way. A bank's logo is optional at Plaid, and a bank without one takes the bank glyph rather than its initials: two letters in a square read as a monogram nobody chose, and four of them down a page read as four. A merchant keeps its initials, having no glyph that would say anything. A bank's mark drops the square's line either way — a logo arrives with an edge of its own, and the glyph has to read as the same kind of thing.

A hairline square rather than a filled tile. Filled, a column of them reads as a column of buttons you could press — and the only thing on a balances block you can press is Reconnect, which is a button and looks like one.

Title

The page's name, over it, on the phone. A tab has no other heading at its top: what the page is about is its title, and the figure it opens on.

On a wide window no page opens on its own name, because the lit row in the sidebar already says where you are. On the phone the dock is at the foot, under the thumb and out of the eye's way, so the name goes where the eye starts.

Band

What a page's headline becomes once it has scrolled away: one line of it, over the top of the list. The Ledger's is what its filter is set to, and opens back onto the filter; the others are the figure the page opens on.

It floats over the list rather than pushing it down: a list that moves by the band's height the moment the band arrives jumps under a finger that is already moving. It arrives as the last of the headline goes and leaves a little later than that, so the two cannot chase each other. No panel and nothing floating — the rule under it is what separates it, as it separates every part of a page.

Dock

The tabs, on a bar floating over the foot of the screen, and search on a square of its own beside it. Glyphs only, no words. The lit tab sits on one tile, which slides to whichever tab is lit.

One tile that travels rather than four that come and go, so a change of tab is something you see move. It is a square the height of the row — the words are gone, so what it marks is a glyph, and a lozenge cut for a label under one would sit round the icon with nothing in the rest of it. The bar is a panel and nothing else — its own ground, the chip's corner, and a shadow that says it is above the page. No outline: at this size the shadow is enough, and a line round it only fought the pill inside. It used to be frosted glass, which is a look borrowed from one phone's operating system into a stylesheet three platforms read, and nothing else here is made of it. The words under the glyphs are gone because four tabs that never move are learned once and the labels were read once and skipped past ever after; each name stays in the markup for anything that reads the page aloud. The list still fades out under the bar, so no row is caught half-showing in the gutter either side. Search is drawn and dead, as it is on the phone: nothing has drawn the screen behind it.
