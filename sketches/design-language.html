Design language

Every control Meridian draws, once, so the phone, the web and the desktop app draw the same thing. The styles are in sketches/shared/components.css and the colours in sketches/shared/theme.css; nothing on this page restyles a component, so if one looks wrong here it is wrong.

Two ideas run through all of it. Anything you can select is a frame: an outer hairline, three pixels of ground, and the fill inset inside that — so the selected state is a thing sitting in a frame, not a thing whose border changed colour. Anything that does something stands on a solid line of its own darker shade and drops onto it when pressed. If it stands up it does something; if it lies flat it says something.

Everything is scoped to .ui here so new controls can be drawn beside old ones without the two fighting. In the app there is no such scope — the class names are the components.

Three jobs, and three greys

Violet is you: what you picked, where you are, what day it is. Lime and rose are money in and money owed, and only ever on a figure — never on the word beside it, which already says the direction. Every other thing on the screen is one of three greys.

The app used to carry sixteen category hues as well, one per Plaid primary, in filled capsules down the left of the ledger. They came out: every screen that used one says the category in words in the same place the colour used to sit, and the palette above is what is left. Categorical hues are for a chart where categories are being compared, not for labelling a row.

The glow

Anything holding the highlight colour sits in a little of it — --glow, a short shadow falling downward. Small enough that you would not name it if asked what changed.

It falls downward rather than haloing because a blurred shadow at no offset rounds the corners off: a rectangle wearing one reads as a lozenge, dimmer at the corners than along the edges. And it goes on single things, never on a row of them — nine glowing chips is nine little lamps.

a section of a page the next section of the page a row, or a group of them the next row, or group

Two weights, both full width

.rule-sec between the parts of a page, .rule-row between rows and between groups of them. Both run to the edge of the screen.

A rule that stops short of the edge reads as a third kind of line, which is how a page ends up looking like it has three systems in it. Rows you read down rather than compare across — transactions, accounts — get space and a heading instead of either.

Selecting

Chip

The frame, at its usual size. Selected is the accent tint with the label in violet-300 and the border warmed by the same amount. A count goes at the left of the label, in the money face. .sm is the same thing smaller, for a summary rather than a control you aim at; .dead is drawn but not built.

.chip > .in > (.n, label) · .on · .sm · .dead

Chip row

Where the order of a row of chips carries no meaning, it is chosen to fill the measure, and the space between them takes up whatever is left so each line is flush at both edges.

Every partition of the chips into lines is costed by the square of what each line wastes and the cheapest is taken. Greedy widest-first gets close and then leaves one line short, which is a ragged edge you can see from across the room. The chips keep their own widths — stretching them makes “Travel” as wide as “Transportation”, and a chip's width should say something about its label.

This is the core's job, not the view's: the platform receives the order already packed, like every other finished value.

Range

One outline, the options inside it, the live one filled — the web's 1D/1W/1M selector, and the same control stood on its end. Every option the same height. .tok is an editable word inside one.

The UI of an enum. Outlining each option separately makes four controls sitting near each other rather than one control with four settings, and rows of different heights say one case is bigger than another, which in an enum it is not.

Acting

Button

Raised on a solid line of its own darker shade, and it drops onto that line when pressed. Solid violet is one action per screen: the thing you are there to do. Everything else is the quiet one.

The edge has to be darker than the button and lighter than the page, or it disappears against one of them — which is why the quiet button sits a step up from the ground it stands on rather than flush with it. The violet one carries two steps of the same violet, enough to catch a highlight across the top and not enough to read as a gradient.

Button group

The same buttons with the gap taken out: shared side borders, and the edges running into one line under the row. The live one is the violet button exactly.

A row of flat panels with one of them filled is a segment, which is a different control that happens to look similar. This is a row of buttons with one of them pressed, and it should keep saying so.

Plain button

No frame and no edge, so it presses down a pixel of its own. For an action that has to be available without being the loudest thing in its row — Reconnect, sitting in a list of bank names.

An icon here is worth its space when it says something the label cannot: “open in new” on Reconnect is true, because Plaid Link in update mode is a hosted page and the tap leaves the app.

Share

A proportion is drawn on the rule under the thing it belongs to: square ends, standing on the line rather than straddling it, and starting where the words start.

Reading as part of the line rather than as a bar laid over it means a list of shares costs no height at all beyond the rules it already had. The smallest share still gets a visible nub — a share you cannot see is a row that looks broken.

Money

Every figure is monospaced with tabular numerals, so a column lines up on the decimal without the reader having to look for it. Lime for in, rose for owed, and both on the figure only.

The word carries the direction, so the figure does not need a sign as well: “$4.22 in”, not “+$4.22 in”. And a colour is worth having only where it is scarce — six rose figures under a heading that already says Owed is the heading said seven times, so the heading keeps the colour and the rows go plain.

Day

Plain, marked, today, tapped, outside the month. Today is the accent as a wash; a tapped day is filled in grey. Marks say something landed and never say what.

A tapped day is filled rather than outlined because an outline reads as a different kind of control sitting in the grid. The marks stay neutral because whatever is above the calendar has already named the categories, in those colours, a screenful higher up — the calendar's question is when.

The class is .picked and not .sel: .sel is already the editable word in a range sentence, and a .cal-day.sel quietly inherits that token's padding and corner.
